\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Spatial transcriptomics is measured on platforms that differ in almost every respect. Imaging-based assays such as
Xenium and CosMx resolve single cells against targeted panels. Sequencing-based assays such as Visium HD and
Stereo-seq capture larger gene sets on grids whose resolution and diffusion depend on the chemistry. When serial
sections of one tissue are profiled on several platforms~\todo{SPATCH citation}, the same cell types land in
different regions of an embedding space depending on how they were measured. Foundation
models~\citep{cui2024scgpt,wang2025scgptspatial,tejadalapuerta2025nicheformer} give every platform a common
vocabulary, but not a common geometry.

A common workflow annotates one high-quality reference and brings other data sets into it. Most integration
methods cannot support this. Harmony~\citep{korsunsky2019harmony}, Scanorama~\citep{hie2019scanorama} and spatially
aware methods such as GraphST~\citep{long2023graphst} return a new embedding for all inputs. The reference moves, and
every classifier, index or annotation built on it must be rebuilt. USHER~\citep{pratapa2025usher} instead keeps the
target fixed and learns a low-capacity map of the source into it, using fused Gromov--Wasserstein (FGW) transport to
find correspondences. Its structure matrices, however, are recomputed in every EM iteration, and this dominates its
run time. Composite integration scores are a poor guide to such methods. They are dominated by batch mixing, which
moment matching recovers cheaply and over-correction can inflate, so we read results as a trade-off between mixing
and conservation of biological structure.

Our contributions are:
(i) \frost, a post-hoc, reference-preserving FGW method that builds the structure matrices once per batch and caches them
($2B$ instead of $2BT$ computations for $B$ batches and $T$ iterations) and ends with closed-form moment matching;
(ii) a benchmark of 12 source--reference pairs from three tumour types and four source platforms, against ten
baselines, including a measure of reference drift;
(iii) evidence that a spatially aware integration method fails in this setting, transferring labels worse than no
alignment while displacing its reference;
(iv) a comparison with USHER showing that the two reach the same total score from opposite sides of the trade-off,
with 25-fold fewer structure computations for \frost; and
(v) a mechanistic account of why global embedding alignment blurs gene-level neighbourhoods, which sets the scope of
the method.
