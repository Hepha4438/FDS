\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Spatial transcriptomics is now measured on platforms that differ in almost every respect. Imaging-based assays such
as Xenium and CosMx resolve single cells against targeted panels of a few thousand genes. Sequencing-based assays
such as Visium HD and Stereo-seq capture larger gene sets on grids of bins whose size and diffusion profile depend
on the chemistry. Data sets that profile serial sections of the same tissue on several platforms~\todo{SPATCH
citation} make the consequence visible: the same cell types land in different places of an embedding space
depending on how they were measured. Foundation models trained on single-cell and spatial
data~\citep{cui2024scgpt,wang2025scgptspatial,tejadalapuerta2025nicheformer} give every platform a common
vocabulary, but not a common geometry.

A common way to work across platforms is to annotate one high-quality reference and bring every other data set into
it. Most integration methods do not support this. Harmony~\citep{korsunsky2019harmony} and
Scanorama~\citep{hie2019scanorama} return a new embedding for all inputs. Spatially aware methods such as
GraphST~\citep{long2023graphst} and STAligner~\citep{zhou2023staligner} train a new encoder on all sections. In
both cases the reference moves, and every classifier, label transfer index or annotated atlas built on it must be
rebuilt each time a new data set arrives. Reference-mapping methods such as Symphony~\citep{kang2021symphony} and
scArches~\citep{lotfollahi2022scarches} keep the reference fixed, but they require a reference built in their own
model space rather than the embedding the analyst already uses. USHER~\citep{pratapa2025usher} takes a third route:
it keeps the target embedding fixed and learns a low-capacity map of the source into it, using fused
Gromov--Wasserstein (FGW) transport to find correspondences. Its structure matrices, however, are recomputed in
every EM iteration, and that cost dominates its run time.

We ask what a reference-preserving method buys, and what it costs, when both sides are spatial and come from
different platforms. Composite integration scores are a poor guide here. They are dominated by batch mixing, which
second-order moment matching recovers almost for free, and mixing can reward over-correction. We therefore read
results as a trade-off between mixing and conservation of biological structure, and we add task metrics that probe
label transfer, rare cell types and local neighbourhoods.

Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \frost, a post-hoc, reference-preserving alignment method built on USHER. It computes the FGW structure
  matrices once from frozen inputs ($2B$ instead of $2BT$ computations for $B$ batches and $T$ iterations), fits a
  linear identity-regularised map with a label-vote auxiliary cost, and ends with closed-form moment matching
  (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
  \item A benchmark of 12 source--reference pairs from SPATCH (three tumour types, four source platforms, Xenium 5K
  reference) against ten baselines, with metrics for mixing, conservation, label transfer, rare types and
  microenvironment, and a measure of reference drift (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Evidence that a spatially aware integration method fails in this setting: GraphST transfers labels worse
  than no alignment in 11 of 12 pairs while displacing its own reference (Section~\ref{sec:res_spatial}).
  \item A pipeline-level comparison with the USHER reference implementation. The two methods reach the same total
  score from opposite sides of the mixing--conservation trade-off, and \frost uses 25-fold fewer structure
  computations (Section~\ref{sec:res_usher}).
  \item An explicit boundary: embedding alignment does not improve gene-level tasks, and post-hoc moment matching
  trades local density fidelity for distribution-level agreement (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\end{itemize}
